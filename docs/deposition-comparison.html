The question counsel faces

The seller points to the buyer’s shipping release as support for its final invoice. You have ten minutes to examine Ellis Reed, the seller’s project manager, about site acceptance. How much can Reed establish from personal knowledge, and where would you go for the rest?

Exhibit A · Agreement, p. 3, §§4.1–4.2
The buyer may authorize shipment by email. The final installment comes due on written site acceptance signed by the buyer’s commissioning lead.
Exhibit B · Email chain, p. 1, B1–B2
On June 5 the buyer’s logistics coordinator released the order for shipment and said site testing was the following week. On June 12 Reed wrote, “I haven't seen a signed site acceptance. Records may have the original.”
Exhibit C · Reed testimony, p. 18, lines 3–12
Reed handled dispatch and searched the dispatch folder. Reed had not searched the separate acceptance register, which records lead Dana Ives maintained.

How to run the exercise yourself

  1. Choose one decision and give it a small, checkable record. We asked for four lead questions, pinpoints, foundation and conditional follow-ups. The record contains a meaningful distinction a quick read can miss. A witness’s unsuccessful search tells you about the places the witness actually searched.
  2. Write down what will count before generating answers. Decide which facts a good outline must preserve, what a usable question sounds like, and which error would stop you from using it. We fixed five criteria worth two points each. Full scoring anchors · Pre-run packet and rubric.
  3. Change only the skill. Pick one deposition skill in each of three fresh chats. Use the same model, effort, packet, directions and output format. Give each skill its own bundled materials. Keep other answers, rubrics, playbooks and connected matter sources out of the drafting chats. Save the first completed answer from each scored run.
  4. Hide the source and lock the grades. Have someone randomly label the outputs A, B and C and keep the key separately. Preserve the wording. Give two independent readers the packet, scoring anchors and anonymous outlines. Reverse the reading order for the second reader. Ask each to cite a question for every deduction and identify the first edit they would make.
  5. Reveal the names and test the criticism. Reopen the exhibit behind each disputed grade. On a matter of your own, make the edits and time them. A run about one witness can tell you which outline you would use for that examination. A different assignment may reward a different skill.

The anonymous outlines

Read each as if you were about to question Reed. Which question would you ask as written? Mark the first one you would change.

Read Outline A

  1. “Your work on this order was dispatch scheduling, and you were copied on logistics messages, correct?”
    Source/foundation: Reed’s own sworn testimony, Ex. C, p. 18, lines 3–5; establishes his role and opportunity to know.
    If he describes broader responsibilities: “What site-acceptance work did you personally perform?”

  2. “Do you recognize this June 5 email addressed to you, authorizing release for shipment and saying site testing was next week?”
    Source/foundation: Ex. B, p. 1, B1; establish recognition and receipt before exploring his understanding.
    If he treats it as site acceptance: “What did you understand to constitute acceptance signed by Buyer’s commissioning lead, and what supported that understanding?”
    Counsel note: This is the seller’s strongest document. Ex. A, p. 3, §4.1 permits shipment release by email; §4.2 ties the final installment to written site acceptance signed by the commissioning lead. Establish Reed’s factual understanding without assuming he administered those terms.

  3. “Did you send the June 12 email reporting that you had not seen a signed site acceptance and that records might have the original, after searching your dispatch folder without searching the separate acceptance register?”
    Source/foundation: Ex. B, p. 1, B2, establishes authorship and the complete qualification; Reed’s own testimony, Ex. C, p. 18, lines 6–9, establishes the search’s limits.
    If denied or qualified: “Which part needs correcting—the email’s authorship, the locations you searched, or both?”
    Counsel note: These passages are consistent. Neither establishes that no signed acceptance existed.

  4. “You previously identified records lead Dana Ives as maintaining the separate acceptance register; what do you personally know about where that register and any original signed acceptance could be found?”
    Source/foundation: Reed’s own testimony, Ex. C, p. 18, lines 10–12, including his lack of knowledge of the register’s contents; Ex. B, p. 1, B2, identifies records as a possible location for the original.
    If he cannot identify a location: “Who would know where those records are kept?” If he now claims knowledge of their contents: “What did you personally see, and when?”
    Counsel note: Best remaining proof: the acceptance register and any original written acceptance bearing Buyer’s commissioning lead’s signature, including its date. Dana Ives is the identified custodian lead; the packet does not establish what those records contain.

Read Outline B

  1. “Which messages in Exhibit B do you recognize from your own work on this order?”
    Sources: B, p. 1, B1–B2; C, p. 18, lines 3–5.
    If recognition is limited: “Which did you personally receive or send?”
    Counsel note: Establish receipt of B1 and authorship of B2 before relying on them. Reed previously described his role as dispatch scheduling and being copied on logistics messages. 1 minute.

  2. “What, if anything, in the June 5 email did you understand to be written site acceptance signed by the buyer’s commissioning lead?”
    Sources: A, p. 3, §§4.1–4.2; B, p. 1, B1.
    If Reed identifies acceptance: “What words or signature support that understanding, and how did you know the signer was the commissioning lead?”
    Counsel note: B1 expressly releases shipment and places site testing the following week. Keep the inquiry on Reed’s understanding and its factual basis. 3 minutes.

  3. “Before writing on June 12, ‘I haven't seen a signed site acceptance,’ where had you personally searched for one?”
    Sources: B, p. 1, B2; C, p. 18, lines 6–9.
    If Reed now includes the acceptance register: “You previously testified that you searched your dispatch folder but did not search the separate acceptance register. What explains that difference?”
    Counsel note: Establish the search’s limits; failure to find acceptance in his folder does not establish that none existed. 3 minutes.

  4. “Based on your personal knowledge, who could locate any entry for this order in the separate acceptance register and any original signed acceptance?”
    Sources: C, p. 18, lines 10–12; B, p. 1, B2.
    If Reed identifies Dana Ives: “What identifying information would she need to find those records?” If he cannot identify a source: “What was the basis for your statement that Records may have the original?”
    Counsel note: Dana and the register are the principal remaining proof leads. Reed previously said he did not know the register’s contents; stop when his personal knowledge is exhausted. 3 minutes.

Read Outline C

  1. “You wrote to seller billing on June 12, ‘I haven't seen a signed site acceptance,’ correct?”
    Source: Ex. B, p. 1, B2.
    If authorship is denied: “What makes you dispute that this is your email?”
    If he claims to have seen an acceptance: “When did you first see it, and where can we find that document?”

  2. “The June 5 email sent to you said, ‘Release for shipment today. Site testing is next week,’ correct?”
    Source: Ex. B, p. 1, B1.
    If receipt is denied: “Did you otherwise learn of that shipment release?”
    If he treats the email as site acceptance: Show Ex. A, p. 3, §§4.1–4.2; ask, “What writing are you relying on as site acceptance signed by the Buyer’s commissioning lead?”
    Counsel note: Establish the document he relies on; avoid asking Reed to decide the contractual payment obligation.

  3. “In your earlier sworn testimony, you said you did not search the separate acceptance register on June 12, correct?”
    Source: Ex. C, p. 18, lines 6–9.
    If denied: Show the passage; ask, “Did you give that answer under oath?”
    If he describes an additional or later search: “When did you search the register, and what did you find?”
    Counsel note: His stated role was dispatch scheduling and receiving copied logistics messages. His search and personal knowledge do not establish that no acceptance existed. Ex. C, p. 18, lines 3–12.

  4. “You previously identified Dana Ives as maintaining the acceptance register; how can we locate Dana and that register?”
    Source: Ex. C, p. 18, lines 10–12; Ex. B, p. 1, B2 (“Records may have the original.”).
    If he cannot say or identifies a changed custodian: “Who would know the register’s current custodian and location?”
    Counsel note: Seek the order’s register entry and any signed original it identifies, including the signature and date. Reed expressly disclaimed knowledge of the register’s contents; its contents remain unproved.

For a short examination about exhibits and a witness’s personal knowledge, try LegalQuants first. Its last question asks who could locate the register entry and signed original. If Reed names the custodian, the follow-up asks what identifying information she would need. That answer could tell counsel exactly what to request next. Astra tied LegalQuants with Workbench and wanted the shipping authorization established aloud. Sol gave LegalQuants full marks.

What the other two did well. Workbench put Reed’s dispatch role on the record immediately and pursued the missing original. Both judges would break up its third question, which asks Reed to confirm authorship, the substance of an email and the places searched in one answer. Claude asked where to find a document Reed claimed to have seen. It established which register Reed skipped, but left Reed’s dispatch role in counsel’s notes and never asked which folder Reed actually searched.

A defect both graders missed. Both gave all three outlines full credit for source fidelity. Workbench Q3 puts the email after the search. LegalQuants Q3 starts “Before writing on June 12” and invites the same assumption. B2 dates the email. C, p. 18, lines 6–9 dates the search. Neither establishes their order. I would ask, “Where did you look for a signed acceptance on June 12?” Then, “Was that before or after your email to billing?” The published scores are the graders’ original returns. What this run covered

We installed the three source packages into disposable Codex profiles, verified the deposition skill text, and explicitly selected each installed skill through Codex’s native skill-selection API in a fresh, temporary project chat. Each received the identical assignment, with its own workflow instructions and bundled reference material available. GPT-6.1 Sol drafted all three at high reasoning effort. The scored runs used the first completed answer from each skill. We set aside an earlier setup pass before reading its outputs so the scored comparison could include each skill’s bundled templates.

A random draw assigned A, B and C. The outlines are unedited. Two fresh grading chats received only the packet, scorecard and anonymous answers. GPT-6 Astra read A–B–C and GPT-6.1 Sol read C–B–A, both at high effort. We locked both scorecards before opening the identity key. Sol shares the drafting model. This was one fictional, text-only individual-witness exercise with AI graders. We did not measure lawyer editing time, connected discovery search, corporate-designee prep or transcript analysis.

What changed with the Workbench revision?

The old Workbench averaged 9.44. The revision gained 0.31 points on the unrounded means and went from seven outlines needing a repair to five. Across the eight record-and-run pairs it scored higher five times and tied three times. Both versions located the central proof gaps in many answers. Their largest score difference was on freight, where the emails actually did establish the order of approval and confirmation.

  • What improved. Both revised freight outlines kept the 08:12 approval, 08:19 booking message and 08:32 confirmation in order. Both warned counsel that those timestamps still did not establish the instant the carrier actually booked the slot. The old first run labeled its objective “Approval before commitment.” Its spoken question supported a narrower proposition. Compare freight run 1, outlines A and C.
  • Where a second read pays off. On the supplier presentation, a LegalQuants follow-up asked which setting the presenter remembered communicating after a qualified answer. The witness might remember showing only the yield chart. Revised Workbench’s second run distinguished remembering that no settings were communicated from having no recollection either way. Its first run still assumed the AV recording had sound. Compare presentation run 2, outlines A and B, and run 1, outline D.
  • Where all three stumbled. The vendor moved a promised sample delivery from Monday to Thursday. The sales director said this affected plans for a buyer meeting. None of the eight retail outlines escaped a repair. Some asked what the buyer said about missing mock-ups before establishing what happened at the meeting. Others assumed the director supplied the CRM loss reason. The buyer’s own email cited a margin exception. Read the five retail exhibits.

One pass before taking an outline into the room. For each consequential follow-up, give the witness a plausible answer that qualifies the lead. Does the follow-up still make sense? On retail, ask “Did the meeting take place? What did you show? Was anything you intended to show missing?” Only if something was missing, ask whether the buyer commented and what the buyer said. Check counsel’s objective and the requested proof against the same exhibits.

How the comparison worked

We fixed the packets, rubric and decision rule before drafting. For each record, we ran LegalQuants Litigation, Claude for Legal and the revised Workbench deposition skill twice. We also ran the previous Workbench skill twice to test the effect of the revision. That gave us 32 outlines across four records. All drafting chats used GPT-6.1 Sol at high effort, a fresh context and identical exhibits. Codex explicitly selected the versioned skill from a temporary project. Each skill had its own bundled Markdown references available. The drafting instructions contained no timing checklist, answer key or research tools.

Within each set of four, the harness drew new anonymous letters. Two independent grading chats read the answers in opposite orders. GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol each awarded up to two points for five things: addressing the client’s decision and its strongest objection, fidelity to the source, witness foundation, the next proof to obtain and usefulness at the deposition. A separate review checked consequential factual premises against the exhibits while identities were still hidden. The scores and the source-audit decisions are reported separately.

To change the starting choice, a challenger had to lead LegalQuants by at least one point out of ten overall, finish ahead on at least three records and have no more outlines with a verified material premise to repair. Verified defects in LegalQuants on two or more records called for a qualified recommendation even if a challenger missed that bar. The older Workbench version served only as a control. Frozen scoring rules and answer key · Pre-run commit.

A separate Astra chat for each record inspected all eight anonymous outlines. The coordinating AI checked the proposed defects against the exhibits, added or rejected findings with reasons, and locked those decisions before decoding the letters. No lawyer graded the answers. The 32 drafts are repeated runs on four constructed, text-only fact-witness examinations. These data do not measure attorney editing time, effectiveness at a live deposition, corporate-designee preparation or work with a connected discovery platform.
